## Is washing machine water clean or contaminated?

It depends on where the failure happened, and it changes how the carpet and pad get treated. A burst supply hose is Category 1 water, meaning it came from a clean, pressurized line, the same classification used in the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for a broken pipe or faucet line.

An overflow from the machine itself, where wash water backs up out of the standpipe or drain, is Category 2 gray water. That water carries detergent, soil, and sometimes grease from the wash cycle. Category 2 water is why carpet pad under a laundry room often gets replaced instead of dried in place, even when the carpet itself can be saved. Clean water from a hose failure has more drying options; gray water from an overflow usually does not.

Don't guess which one you have. Tell whoever answers the phone whether the hose was disconnected or still attached and whether the water smelled or looked soapy. That detail changes the scope of the job before anyone arrives.

## What should I do in the first 10 minutes?

Shut off both water supply valves behind the machine first, then unplug the unit at the wall. Most washers sit on a pair of hot and cold valves mounted in the wall or a recessed box; turning both clockwise stops the source even if the hose itself failed.

After the water is off, mop or wet-vac what you can reach, especially around baseboards where it wants to wick into the wall. Photograph the standing water, the hose or connection that failed, and any flooring that's already starting to lift or buckle. That documentation matters for your insurance claim and for whoever scopes the drying job. Then make the call. Don't wait to see if it dries on its own, especially on laminate or vinyl plank, which trap water underneath instead of letting it evaporate.

## Where does the water spread that I can't see?

Washing machine water travels under flooring and into wall cavities long before it's visible on the surface. Laminate and vinyl plank are the worst offenders: water wicks between planks and pools underneath, where it sits against the subfloor out of sight. By the time you see cupping or lifting at the seams, the subfloor has often been wet for hours.

In an open-concept layout common in newer Rosedale and Riverlakes Ranch homes, water also travels sideways into an adjoining carpeted family room or hallway, soaking the pad before the carpet itself looks wet. And if the laundry is upstairs, which is common in two-story Stockdale Estates and Seven Oaks plans, gravity does the rest: water finds its way into the floor cavity and shows up as a stain on the ceiling below, sometimes a day later. A moisture meter reading at the baseboard, not a visual check, is the only reliable way to know how far it actually spread.

## Can I just dry it myself with fans and towels?

A box fan and open windows are enough only if the water is caught within the first hour and never touched drywall, carpet pad, or a wall cavity. Past that point, surface drying leaves hidden moisture that [EPA guidance on mold and moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) notes can start growing visible mold within 24 to 48 hours.

Call a professional if water sat for more than a few hours before you found it, if drywall or carpet pad got wet, or if the leak happened in a second-story laundry room where you can't see what's happening in the floor cavity below. A restoration crew uses a moisture meter to map the actual wet area, not just what's visible, then sets extraction and drying equipment sized to the room, not a rental shop vac and a fan from the garage. The single best prevention step for next time: replace rubber washing machine hoses with braided stainless steel ones. Rubber hoses are the most common point of failure and typically need replacing every five years; braided steel hoses resist the bursts that cause most of these floods in the first place.
